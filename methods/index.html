Fields and sign conventions

Each window is an independent celeri block model with triangular fault meshes, estimated by MCMC. The viewer shows posterior means (or standard deviations) on each mesh triangle:

  • Elastic slip deficit — the slip-deficit rate that produces the elastic part of the surface velocity field (mm/yr). In coseismic windows it is the slip deficit accumulated over the window, in mm. Positive dip-slip is a reverse-sense deficit (locking); coseismic slip and afterslip on thrust faults are therefore negative. The “show as slip” option flips the sign.
  • Kinematic slip — the slip rate implied by the relative motion of the bounding blocks, smoothed onto the mesh (mm/yr). Positive strike-slip is left-lateral, positive dip-slip is reverse.
  • Coupling — elastic slip deficit divided by kinematic slip, where the mesh is parameterized by coupling (0 = freely slipping, 1 = fully locked). It is drawn in viridis on [0, 1]; in the default experiments the dipping crustal meshes allow dip-slip coupling down to −10, and values below 0 are drawn dark. Experiments that allow negative coupling everywhere it matters (the starter models' postseismic host meshes: [−2, 1], or [−10, 1] on the Japan Trench in “Japan Trench afterslip to −10” and all later experiments, with [−50, 1] in the scaled-BEL experiment's first year after Tohoku, where values below −10 are drawn at the blue end; at −2 the fault slips at three times the block-motion rate) use a red–blue map instead, with 0 white at the centre, the lower bound at the blue end and 1 at the red end.
  • Apparent coupling — coupling where the mesh estimates it; elsewhere in rate windows (meshes in elastic, free-slip mode after a large earthquake) the elastic slip deficit divided by the kinematic rate. The derived values are a way to read free slip on the same footing as coupling: afterslip faster than the plate rate appears as strongly negative apparent coupling. They are masked where the kinematic rate is below 1 mm/yr. Their SD is the elastic SD divided by the kinematic rate. The readout says, for each patch, whether the value is estimated or derived.

Rate windows use a red–blue map (red positive); coseismic windows use a purple–orange map and their own scale, because they are displacements in mm rather than rates. The viewer opens on a fixed linear scale; the logarithmic option, symmetric about zero, makes interseismic rates of a few mm/yr and early afterslip of tens of m/yr readable together.

Time windows

The GEONET record (2005-07-21 to 2025-05-09) is cut at Global CMT earthquakes of Mw ≥ 6.5 that are close enough to offset at least one station (distance ≤ 100.36 Mw − 0.15 km, surface distance plus depth). Each such event, or cluster of events within two days, becomes a coseismic window. Coseismic slip is modeled only for earthquakes that are on a modeled fault: the hypocenter within 30 km of a mesh and one Global CMT nodal plane within 20° of the local subduction-interface plane (40° for the schematic crustal faults). Intraslab, outer-rise and upper-plate events, and clusters with fewer than 10 stations inside the offset radius, still split the record but get no model run (hatched on the timeline).

After on-fault events of Mw ≥ 7, windows end 7, 30, 91 and 182 days and 1 year (and 2 years for Mw ≥ 8) after the event, and are labeled postseismic: the host fault is in free-slip mode for 1 year after Mw 7–8 events and 2 years after Mw ≥ 8 events. (The first experiment, “default”, used distance-only hosts and 2-year postseismic periods for every Mw ≥ 7 event.) Remaining long windows are split where many stations share a change in slope (adaptive splitting). In equal-time playback every window gets the same screen time; in calendar playback screen time is proportional to the window's duration, with a minimum so 1-day coseismic windows stay visible.

GNSS velocities and displacements

Most experiments use GSI's F5 daily solutions; experiments labelled UNR use the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory's IGS20 solutions for the same stations. About 600 GEONET stations with continuous daily positions are fit jointly, one least-squares problem per station, with a linear rate in each window (positions continuous across ordinary window boundaries), a step at each coseismic cluster, steps at equipment changes and smaller earthquakes, and annual plus semiannual terms. The daily network-wide common-mode error is estimated and removed first. The window rates are rotated into the frame of a regional velocity field with one fixed rotation and translation; regional stations outside Japan provide far-field constraints in rate windows. In coseismic windows the data are the fitted coseismic steps (mm); stations outside the offset radius contribute their residual jump, which is close to zero.

Known issues

East components are 18–37% too large in the GSI-based experiments (every experiment not labelled UNR). GSI's F5 files give positions as latitude and longitude, and our conversion to east displacements left out the cos(latitude) factor, so east is too large by 1/cos(latitude): ×1.18 at 32° N, ×1.23 at 36° N and ×1.37 at 43° N. North and up are not affected. The rotation into the regional frame absorbs much of the error in the absolute velocities, but east–west changes from window to window (postseismic motion, transients, coseismic steps) are exaggerated by that factor, and the models fit them as they are.

The experiments labelled UNR use the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory's solutions and are not affected. Their network-wide common-mode motion is also two to four times smaller than GSI's, so their short windows are less noisy.

Model in each window

  • Interseismic: fault meshes parameterized by coupling in [0, 1]; the BEL sheet is effectively off.
  • Postseismic: in the default and on-fault experiments, the meshes that hosted the triggering earthquake switch to elastic (free-slip) mode, with a prior scaled to the observed velocities, so afterslip is not tied to block motion; in the starter experiments they stay in coupling mode with a negative lower bound (see Coupling above). The BEL sheet is on.
  • Coseismic: block rotations held at zero and only the host meshes slip.
  • New Noto mesh (“new Noto mesh with free reverse afterslip”): after the 2024 Noto earthquake the Noto–Niigata mesh's dip-slip is in elastic mode but restricted to reverse slip (its slip deficit is bounded above by 0), and its strike-slip stays in coupling mode within [−10, 1]. Without the reverse-only bound, free slip put a third or more of the moment at the mesh's far northeastern end, fitting non-tectonic signal on the Niigata coast.
  • Scaled BEL (“BEL scaled to the data after Tohoku”): the new-Noto-mesh experiment with two changes after Tohoku. From March 2011 to the end of 2023 the BEL sheet's prior σ is half of each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal (2.4 m/yr in the first week, 221 mm/yr in mid-2011, 25 mm/yr in 2022) instead of 5 mm/yr, so slip on the sheet can stand in for the displacements that mantle flow applies to the base of the elastic layer (Loveless & Meade, 2025). In the first year the Japan Trench coupling can reach −50, with a steeper sigmoid so that the prior between 0 and −9 is unchanged. This fits the inland postseismic motion that the other experiments miss, but onshore data cannot tell BEL slip beneath the forearc from deep afterslip: here the BEL slips fastest beneath the forearc, and the trench afterslip is several times smaller than in the other experiments.
  • BEL cut (“BEL cut under the forearc”): the scaled-BEL experiment with two changes after Tohoku. The BEL sheet is trimmed where it lay above the Japan Trench mesh in map view, so it now ends along the line where the slab reaches 50 km depth (the trench mesh's bottom edge), about 200 km from the trench. Published models of Tohoku put a cold, elastic corner of the mantle wedge (the “cold nose”) beneath the forearc, where no mantle flow is expected. In the first year the Japan Trench's dip-slip is in elastic mode, restricted to reverse slip, with a prior σ equal to each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal, instead of the coupling floor of −50. The fit is as good as the scaled-BEL model's, the trench afterslip reaches Mw 8.4 within two years, close to published estimates, and the BEL slips fastest beneath the volcanic arc. Caveat: about 1 m of first-year afterslip lies inside the main rupture near the trench, where seafloor geodesy and repeating earthquakes indicate little afterslip; onshore stations cannot resolve slip there.
  • Zero trench edge (“zero slip deficit at the Nankai and Japan Trench edges”): the BEL-cut experiment with the slip deficit held at zero on the shallowest row of triangles (about 15 km wide) of the Nankai and Japan Trench meshes, in every window. The trench edge therefore creeps at the block-motion rate and has no afterslip, which could hold if the large shallow slip in 2011 was dynamic overshoot (Ide, Baltay and Beroza, 2011, Science 332). In the first year after Tohoku the tolerance is 1% of each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal instead of 0.5 mm/yr, which the sampler could not handle there. It moves about 0.4 m of first-year afterslip out of the main rupture with no change in fit, and calms window-to-window changes at the trench edge. It does not steady the slip farther downdip: before Tohoku, coupling at 25–50 km rises to make up for the pinned edge. The fit is worse at the Suruga Trough, where stations are 20–35 km from the trench edge and the data favour locking right at the trench.

Within an experiment the mesh geometry is the same in every window. The new-Noto-mesh experiment and those built on it use an updated mesh set: the Noto–Niigata mesh reaches 25 km instead of 15 km, and the BEL sheet is at 30 km instead of 50 km (trimmed under the Japan Trench forearc in the BEL-cut and zero-trench-edge experiments).

Uncertainty and convergence

The posterior SD of each component is computed from all MCMC draws (two chains × 300 draws). For the magnitude, the SD is that of the per-draw magnitude. These are exploratory runs: split R-hat is above 1.1 for some parameters in about half the windows, mostly the eigenmode coefficients of meshes in elastic mode in coseismic and postseismic windows. Windows with poor convergence are marked on the timeline and in the readout. Longer runs are planned.
