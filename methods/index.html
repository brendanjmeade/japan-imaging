Fields and sign conventions

Each window is an independent celeri block model with triangular fault meshes, estimated by MCMC. The viewer shows posterior means (or standard deviations) on each mesh triangle:

  • Elastic slip deficit — the slip-deficit rate that produces the elastic part of the surface velocity field (mm/yr). In coseismic windows it is the slip deficit accumulated over the window, in mm. Positive dip-slip is a reverse-sense deficit (locking); coseismic slip and afterslip on thrust faults are therefore negative. The “show as slip” option flips the sign.
  • Kinematic slip — the slip rate implied by the relative motion of the bounding blocks, smoothed onto the mesh (mm/yr). Positive strike-slip is left-lateral, positive dip-slip is reverse.
  • Coupling — elastic slip deficit divided by kinematic slip, where the mesh is parameterized by coupling (0 = freely slipping, 1 = fully locked). It is drawn in viridis on [0, 1]; in the default experiments the dipping crustal meshes allow dip-slip coupling down to −10, and values below 0 are drawn dark. Experiments that allow negative coupling everywhere it matters (the starter models' postseismic host meshes: [−2, 1], or [−10, 1] on the Japan Trench in “Japan Trench afterslip to −10” (0009) and all later experiments, with [−50, 1] in the scaled-BEL experiment's first year after Tohoku, where values below −10 are drawn at the blue end; at −2 the fault slips at three times the block-motion rate) use a red–blue map instead, with 0 white at the centre, the lower bound at the blue end and 1 at the red end.
  • Apparent coupling — coupling where the mesh estimates it; elsewhere in rate windows (meshes in elastic, free-slip mode after a large earthquake) the elastic slip deficit divided by the kinematic rate. The derived values are a way to read free slip on the same footing as coupling: afterslip faster than the plate rate appears as strongly negative apparent coupling. They are masked where the kinematic rate is below 1 mm/yr. Their SD is the elastic SD divided by the kinematic rate. The readout says, for each patch, whether the value is estimated or derived.

Rate windows use a red–blue map (red positive); coseismic windows use a purple–orange map and their own scale, because they are displacements in mm rather than rates. The viewer opens on a fixed linear scale; the logarithmic option, symmetric about zero, makes interseismic rates of a few mm/yr and early afterslip of tens of m/yr readable together.

Time windows

The GEONET record (2005-07-21 to 2025-05-09; from 1997-04-01 in 0024–0040) is cut at Global CMT earthquakes of Mw ≥ 6.5 that are close enough to offset at least one station (distance ≤ 100.36 Mw − 0.15 km, surface distance plus depth). Each such event, or cluster of events within two days, becomes a coseismic window. Coseismic slip is modeled only for earthquakes that are on a modeled fault: the hypocenter within 30 km of a mesh and one Global CMT nodal plane within 20° of the local subduction-interface plane (40° for the schematic crustal faults). Intraslab, outer-rise and upper-plate events, and clusters with fewer than 10 stations inside the offset radius, still split the record but get no model run (hatched on the timeline).

After on-fault events of Mw ≥ 7, windows end 7, 30, 91 and 182 days and 1 year (and 2 years for Mw ≥ 8) after the event, and are labeled postseismic: the host fault is in free-slip mode for 1 year after Mw 7–8 events and 2 years after Mw ≥ 8 events. (The first experiment, “default”, used distance-only hosts and 2-year postseismic periods for every Mw ≥ 7 event.) Remaining long windows are split where many stations share a change in slope (adaptive splitting). In equal-time playback every window gets the same screen time; in calendar playback screen time is proportional to the window's duration, with a minimum so 1-day coseismic windows stay visible.

GNSS velocities and displacements

Most experiments use GSI's F5 daily solutions; experiments labelled UNR use the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory's IGS20 solutions for the same stations. About 600 GEONET stations with continuous daily positions (747 in 0024–0040, the sites with data in every year from 1997 to 2025) are fit jointly, one least-squares problem per station, with a linear rate in each window (positions continuous across ordinary window boundaries), a step at each coseismic cluster, steps at equipment changes and smaller earthquakes, and annual plus semiannual terms. The daily network-wide common-mode error is estimated and removed first. The window rates are rotated into the frame of a regional velocity field with one fixed rotation and translation; regional stations outside Japan provide far-field constraints in rate windows. In coseismic windows the data are the fitted coseismic steps (mm); stations outside the offset radius contribute their residual jump, which is close to zero.

Known issues

East components are 18–37% too large in the GSI-based experiments (every experiment not labelled UNR). GSI's F5 files give positions as latitude and longitude, and our conversion to east displacements left out the cos(latitude) factor, so east is too large by 1/cos(latitude): ×1.18 at 32° N, ×1.23 at 36° N and ×1.37 at 43° N. North and up are not affected. The rotation into the regional frame absorbs much of the error in the absolute velocities, but east–west changes from window to window (postseismic motion, transients, coseismic steps) are exaggerated by that factor, and the models fit them as they are.

The experiments labelled UNR use the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory's solutions and are not affected. Their network-wide common-mode motion is also two to four times smaller than GSI's, so their short windows are less noisy.

Model in each window

  • Interseismic: fault meshes parameterized by coupling in [0, 1]; the BEL sheet is effectively off.
  • Postseismic: in the default and on-fault experiments, the meshes that hosted the triggering earthquake switch to elastic (free-slip) mode, with a prior scaled to the observed velocities, so afterslip is not tied to block motion; in the starter experiments they stay in coupling mode with a negative lower bound (see Coupling above). The BEL sheet is on.
  • Coseismic: block rotations held at zero and only the host meshes slip.
  • New Noto mesh (0010, “new Noto mesh with free reverse afterslip”): after the 2024 Noto earthquake the Noto–Niigata mesh's dip-slip is in elastic mode but restricted to reverse slip (its slip deficit is bounded above by 0), and its strike-slip stays in coupling mode within [−10, 1]. Without the reverse-only bound, free slip put a third or more of the moment at the mesh's far northeastern end, fitting non-tectonic signal on the Niigata coast.
  • Scaled BEL (0011, “BEL scaled to the data after Tohoku”): the new-Noto-mesh experiment with two changes after Tohoku. From March 2011 to the end of 2023 the BEL sheet's prior σ is half of each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal (2.4 m/yr in the first week, 221 mm/yr in mid-2011, 25 mm/yr in 2022) instead of 5 mm/yr, so slip on the sheet can stand in for the displacements that mantle flow applies to the base of the elastic layer (Loveless & Meade, 2025). In the first year the Japan Trench coupling can reach −50, with a steeper sigmoid so that the prior between 0 and −9 is unchanged. This fits the inland postseismic motion that the other experiments miss, but onshore data cannot tell BEL slip beneath the forearc from deep afterslip: here the BEL slips fastest beneath the forearc, and the trench afterslip is several times smaller than in the other experiments.
  • BEL cut (0012, “BEL cut under the forearc”): the scaled-BEL experiment with two changes after Tohoku. The BEL sheet is trimmed where it lay above the Japan Trench mesh in map view, so it now ends along the line where the slab reaches 50 km depth (the trench mesh's bottom edge), about 200 km from the trench. Published models of Tohoku put a cold, elastic corner of the mantle wedge (the “cold nose”) beneath the forearc, where no mantle flow is expected. In the first year the Japan Trench's dip-slip is in elastic mode, restricted to reverse slip, with a prior σ equal to each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal, instead of the coupling floor of −50. The fit is as good as the scaled-BEL model's, the trench afterslip reaches Mw 8.4 within two years, close to published estimates, and the BEL slips fastest beneath the volcanic arc. Caveat: about 1 m of first-year afterslip lies inside the main rupture near the trench, where seafloor geodesy and repeating earthquakes indicate little afterslip; onshore stations cannot resolve slip there.
  • Zero trench edge (0013, “zero slip deficit at the Nankai and Japan Trench edges”): the BEL-cut experiment with the slip deficit held at zero on the shallowest row of triangles (about 15 km wide) of the Nankai and Japan Trench meshes, in every window. The trench edge therefore creeps at the block-motion rate and has no afterslip, which could hold if the large shallow slip in 2011 was dynamic overshoot (Ide, Baltay and Beroza, 2011, Science 332). In the first year after Tohoku the tolerance is 1% of each window's 95th-percentile velocity signal instead of 0.5 mm/yr, which the sampler could not handle there. It moves about 0.4 m of first-year afterslip out of the main rupture with no change in fit, and calms window-to-window changes at the trench edge. It does not steady the slip farther downdip: before Tohoku, coupling at 25–50 km rises to make up for the pinned edge. The fit is worse at the Suruga Trough, where stations are 20–35 km from the trench edge and the data favour locking right at the trench.
  • Remeshed BEL (0014, “remeshed BEL sheet”; 0015, “remeshed BEL sheet with full prior”): the BEL-cut experiment on a remeshed 30 km sheet with the same outline, cut under the forearc in the same way. Its boundary was resampled before meshing, giving 2456 triangles with 30–69 km edges instead of 916 with 11–426 km edges (2399 and 716 after the cut): coarser next to the slab and much finer far from the data, where about half of them lie more than 200 km from any station. In 0014 the BEL keeps 273 eigenmodes, which on this sheet hold 91% of the prior variance instead of 99%, and the fit after Tohoku is about 5% worse. 0015 has 837 eigenmodes, which hold 99%, so its BEL prior is the BEL-cut experiment's on a different mesh; it fits slightly better than the BEL-cut experiment. In both, trench afterslip, Nankai coupling and BEL slip near the stations are unchanged, with trench afterslip again reaching Mw 8.39 at two years: the results do not depend on how the sheet is meshed, as long as the eigenmodes keep the prior.
  • Trench-only BEL (0016, “Japan-Trench-only BEL sheet”): 0015 on a remeshed 30 km sheet bounded by the Japan Trench mesh's 30 km contour and by 34.63°N, so there is no BEL beneath Kyushu, Shikoku or the Ryukyus. The triangles that would lie beneath the shallow Philippine Sea slab in Tokai and Kanto are removed. It tests whether the BEL beneath southwest Japan takes up signals that belong elsewhere. The fit is about 5% worse, almost entirely in Kyushu, where the residuals double in every postseismic window with a coherent westward misfit of about 2 mm/yr. The same misfit appears in the interseismic windows of every experiment, where the BEL is off, so the BEL beneath Kyushu absorbs a standing misfit of the block model there rather than a post-Tohoku signal. Without it, shallow coupling on the Nankai Trough drops to make up part of the misfit, from about 0.3 to near 0 off Kyushu. Residuals at the Bungo Channel, Shikoku, Kii and Tokai barely change, so the BEL there was not mainly fitting the Bungo Channel slow slip events.
  • Tohoku-only BEL (0017, “Tohoku-latitudes-only BEL sheet”): 0016 on a 30 km sheet bounded by the Japan Trench mesh's 30 km contour, 35.5°N and 41.5°N, so there is also no BEL beneath Kanto, Tokai, northernmost Tohoku or Hokkaido (938 triangles after the forearc cut; 338 eigenmodes keep 99% of the prior variance). The fit after Tohoku is about 27% worse than 0016's, almost entirely in Hokkaido north of 42.5°N, where the stations move 7–16 mm/yr further west and 2–7 mm/yr further north than the model, a misfit that grows from 2012 to 2025. The interseismic windows, where the BEL is off, show no consistent offset there, so unlike Kyushu's misfit this is a post-Tohoku signal, which the BEL beneath Hokkaido takes up in 0016. Removing the BEL beneath Kanto and Tokai changes little. Trench afterslip reaches Mw 8.35 at two years, with half as much first-year afterslip inside the main rupture.
  • 2025 catalog (0018, “CMT catalog through May 2025”): 0015 with the earthquake catalog extended from the end of 2024 to May 2025, so the steps of the 2025-01-13 Hyuga-nada Mw 6.8 (35–48 mm at the Miyazaki stations) are removed. Before, they stayed in the Jan–May 2025 window and, because the fitted trajectories are continuous across window boundaries, also inflated the Aug–Dec 2024 window: the speed-up after the 2024 Hyuga-nada earthquake at the nearest station was 84 mm/yr and is now 50. Residuals within 100 km of the epicenter drop from 22 to 6 mm/yr in Aug–Dec 2024 and from 72 to 7.5 mm/yr in 2025; all other windows are unchanged.
  • Hyuga-nada afterslip (0019, “−20 floor and 30 km smoothing”): 0018 with two changes on the Nankai Trough mesh after the 2024-08-08 Hyuga-nada Mw 7.1. With the host floor of −2, coupling saturates and the afterslip spreads over about 26,000 km² from the trench to the base of the mesh. 0019 lowers the floor to −20, with a steeper sigmoid so that the prior is unchanged down to −1.5, and shortens the smoothing length of the slip prior from 79 km (44 eigenmodes) to 30 km (501 eigenmodes, 99% of the prior variance). The mesh itself is unchanged: its triangles are about 10 km across, so the smoothing, not the triangle size, limited the patch size. The afterslip becomes a patch of about 3,500 km² at 19–38 km depth, centred 7 km from the Global CMT centroid in Aug–Dec 2024 and moving about 20 km north in 2025, and residuals within 100 km drop from 6.2 to 3.2 mm/yr. In a synthetic test with the same stations, a 30 km patch at the hypocenter is recovered with the 30 km prior but not with the 79 km one.
  • Remeshed Nankai Trough (0020, “Nankai remeshed … length scale that follows the element size”): 0018 with 0019's −20 floor after the Hyuga-nada earthquake, on a Nankai Trough mesh rebuilt with a spatially variable element size. The slab surface is the same; the elements are 5 km across around the Hyuga-nada rupture, 16 km elsewhere and 24 km at the trench (2358 triangles instead of 1902). The smoothing length of the slip prior follows the element size: about 34 km at Hyuga-nada, 79 km elsewhere, as in 0018, and 107 km at the trench, with 79 eigenmodes. On a refined mesh with one fixed smoothing length the afterslip spreads just as on the old mesh, so the refinement matters only through this local smoothing. In Aug–Dec 2024 the afterslip forms a patch of about 3,700 km² at 17–42 km depth, as in 0019, and residuals within 100 km are 3.4 mm/yr (0019: 3.2). Unlike in 0019, coupling off the Kii Peninsula is unchanged (0.51, against 0.43 in 0019). In a synthetic test the 30 km patch is recovered about as well as with 0019's prior. Before August 2024 the fit in every window is within 1.2% of 0018's, and Nankai coupling changes by a median of 0.27 posterior SD.
  • Coarser trench elements (0021, “Nankai and Japan Trench remeshed with elements that grow away from the stations”): 0020 with both trench meshes remeshed so that the element size follows the resolution of the onshore network: the previous size within 60 km of the nearest station (16 km on the Nankai Trough, 12 km on the Japan Trench), growing in proportion to the distance beyond that, up to 40 km. Distance tracks resolution better than depth: the Japan Trench is 100–220 km from any station along its whole length, while the Nankai Trough comes within 10 km of the stations at the Suruga Trough, which keeps its small elements. The Japan Trench mesh has 1664 triangles instead of 3048, with elements about 30 km across at the trench and the same lower edge, and keeps its fixed 64 km smoothing length (with area-weighted eigenmodes). On the Nankai Trough the smoothing length follows the element size as in 0020. The results match 0020's: the fit in every window is within 0.3%, coupling changes by a median of less than 0.1 posterior SD, and Tohoku afterslip is unchanged. In tests, letting the Japan Trench's smoothing length follow its larger trench elements (about 156 km there) moved poorly resolved postseismic slip to the trench without improving the fit, so that is not used.
  • Back to 1997 (0024, “extended back to 1997 on a fixed 747-site network”): the Boso slow slip events are removed as in 0022–0023, and the record starts in April 1997 instead of July 2005. To keep the station set the same through the record, only the 747 GEONET sites with data in every year from 1997 to 2025 are used (about 600 more sites start later or stop earlier). Before 2005 the windows are about 790 days long up to the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake, then 30, 91, 182 and 365 days after it, with free reverse dip-slip on the Japan Trench for the first 91 days. The 2000 Usu eruption, the 2000 Miyake-jima and 2002 Hachijo-jima dike intrusions and a 2002 Boso slow slip event are removed as steps. After 2005 the results match the 2005–2025 models. Daily positions before about 2003 are noisier (1.5 against 1.1 mm scatter about the fitted trajectories). 0025 and 0026 apply the time smoothing of 0022 and 0023 to these windows, with an added afterslip state for Tokachi-oki. 0027 and 0028 confine the Tokachi-oki and Tohoku afterslip to the trench near each rupture (the full prior over 120 and 450 km along the trench, falling off over 100 km beyond), which removes afterslip off Hokkaido in the first months after Tohoku without changing the fit, and add an afterslip state for the 1994 Sanriku-haruka-oki earthquake, which is already decaying when the record starts. It fits the rise in coupling off Sanriku from 1997 to 2003 as well as a change in background coupling does. 0029 and 0030 also smooth the Tohoku afterslip and keep it down dip of the poorly resolved trench edge: its prior has its own 150 km smoothing length instead of the mesh's 64 km and is cut to 20% shallower than 5 km, rising to full strength at 20 km. This removes short-wavelength patches near the trench, halves the share of first-year afterslip shallower than 15 km (to 31% and 25%), lowers Tohoku afterslip by about 0.1 Mw and worsens the fit after Tohoku by 1.5–2% (median). 0031 and 0032 let 0030's background coupling change more between windows. In 0031 a change larger than half its prior SD over a patch about 100 km across costs only logarithmically more, so a change the data demand is no longer shrunk away. Windows shorter than 180 days, and places where an afterslip state's prior is larger than the background's, keep the Gaussian prior; otherwise early postseismic signal and part of the Tohoku afterslip go into the background. The background then changes in 11 patch-windows: the Bungo Channel during the 2010 and 2018–19 slow slip events, central Shikoku in 2022–23, and the deep southern Japan Trench in 2007–09, where the 2008 Ibaraki-oki (Mw 6.8) and Fukushima-oki (Mw 6.9) earthquakes, not modeled as events, may contribute afterslip. Elsewhere it matches 0030, with the same fit. 0032 doubles the background's drift everywhere (0.2 instead of 0.1 over 5 years). Changes between windows on the Nankai Trough double, the fit improves by 1.8% (median), and after Tohoku the background off Miyagi and Sanriku is 0.05–0.17 lower than in 0030, a split with the afterslip that the priors decide. Cross-validation across windows does not clearly favour either.
  • Smaller earthquakes and the trench edge (0033–0036): 0033 adds afterslip states for the six Japan Trench earthquakes of Mw 6.8–7.0 (2003 off Miyagi, 2004 Kushiro-oki, 2008 Ibaraki-oki and Fukushima-oki, 2015 and 2021 off Miyagi), whose afterslip the larger states and the background otherwise take up. Each falls inside a window, and its state starts in that window, weighted by the part of the window after the earthquake. The Kushiro-oki state takes a patch off eastern Hokkaido in 2004–05 that 0032 gave to Tokachi-oki, the 2008 states take about half of the 2008 drop in deep southern Japan Trench coupling, and the fit and Tohoku afterslip are unchanged. 0034 and 0035 test a zero-slip trench edge on the Japan Trench and Nankai Trough meshes. 0034 holds every afterslip state at zero on the top row of triangles; 0035 holds the total slip deficit there at zero, as celeri's 0013 did. Both lower window cross-validation, 0034 almost entirely in the two years after Tohoku, where the onshore data favour some afterslip at the trench edge, and 0035 by twice as much, with a fit 5% worse (28% at the Suruga Trough). 0036 keeps the Tokachi-oki afterslip around its rupture. In 0027–0035 its prior was confined along the trench from the earthquake's nearest point on the trench edge, which for an earthquake about 100 km landward of the junction of the Japan and Kuril trenches fell on the junction's corner, so the full prior reached northern Sanriku and the afterslip formed a separate patch there. With a Gaussian falloff over 80 km around the Global CMT centroid instead, the afterslip is one connected patch in every window, with the same fit.
  • Reference-frame error and afterslip near its source (0037): 0036 with three changes. First, each window gets a reference-frame error: a rotation of the GEONET network, estimated with the model and marginalized, with a prior SD twice the window's noise SD. The windows after the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake have velocity offsets shared by the whole network (October–December 2003: about 13 mm/yr south and 7 mm/yr east in every region from Kyushu to Hokkaido; the next window the reverse). Afterslip far offshore, whose surface velocities are nearly uniform, had partly absorbed them, most visibly as a patch near the trench off Miyagi in the state of the 2003-10-31 Mw 7.0 earthquake. Second, the Tokachi-oki afterslip prior falls off over 50 km from the centroid instead of 80 km, so 80% of the afterslip moment lies within 100 km of it, peaking at 21 km depth instead of 14. Third, the smaller Japan Trench afterslip states get Tohoku's depth taper. Window cross-validation favours the frame term clearly; the tighter Tokachi-oki prior costs a third of that gain, almost all in the first month after the earthquake, where the held-out data prefer broader afterslip. The fit is about 6% better than 0036's and Tohoku afterslip is unchanged.
  • Block rotations estimated in the record (0038): in every experiment up to 0037 the block rotations are fixed to a reference solve of the 2005–2011 velocities, and in Hokkaido those velocities carry the decade-long transient after the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake: they differ from the 1997–2003 velocities by about 10 mm/yr east-southeast across the whole island, and the reference put about 7 mm/yr of that into Hokkaido's block motion. 0038 makes the rotations of the 15 blocks with GEONET sites static unknowns of the record model (prior SD 10 mm/yr at each block's sites, centred on the reference; the plates fixed in the block file stay fixed). The kinematic slip rates that turn coupling into slip depend on the rotations, so the problem is solved by repeated linearization (four passes). The rotations are estimated from the windows before Tohoku and held fixed after it: estimated from every window, they took up the long post-Tohoku transient, which the afterslip states do not fully capture, and the fit before 2011 got worse everywhere. Relative to the reference, the small blocks of central Honshu move 4–7 mm/yr east, the Hokkaido and northern Tohoku blocks 1–4 mm/yr, mostly east, and southwest Japan and the Ryukyus 2 mm/yr or less. The estimate does not move Hokkaido back toward its 1997–2003 velocities, even when fit only to the interseismic windows. The record's correlated noise (1.4 mm/yr over 150 km) treats an island-wide offset largely as noise, so the rotations are set mainly by velocity structure shorter than that, and the model still runs about 7 mm/yr east of the 1997–2003 Hokkaido velocities. Window cross-validation is 357 above 0037's and higher in every window, including the post-Tohoku windows the rotations were not fit to; plain residuals are slightly larger before 2011 and smaller after. Background coupling rises by about 0.04 off Sanriku and 0.08 off Miyagi–Fukushima, and Tohoku afterslip is unchanged. A synthetic test recovers a 5 mm/yr change of one Hokkaido block's motion to within 0.4 mm/yr.
  • A slow Tokachi-oki state (interim) (0039): 0038 with a far-field state for the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake on the base-of-elastic-layer sheet: one slip pattern over all of Hokkaido (a 300 km Gaussian taper around the centroid) whose prior decays as an Omori law with c = 1 yr. It takes the island-wide east-southeast motion of the first years after the earthquake, about 11 mm/yr in the first month and 1–2 mm/yr in 2006–2011, and lowers the Hokkaido residual RMS for 2003–2011 from 8.9 to 6.4 mm/yr. While the state is active it replaces the per-window BEL term on the whole sheet, including beneath Kyushu, so southwest Japan fits slightly worse in 2003–2011. The block rotations, re-estimated from the 1997–2011 windows, barely change, and the model still runs 6–7 mm/yr east of the 1997–2003 Hokkaido velocities; with a slower decay (c = 5 yr) the state shrinks and the first year fits worse. Window cross-validation is 349 above 0038's and higher in every window: the gains of the state and of the estimated rotations nearly add. An interim model while time-varying block rotations are tested.
  • Time-varying Hokkaido block rotations (0040): 0039 with a transient change of the okhotsk and kuril-north blocks' rotations after the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake. In each window from the earthquake on, a block rotates at its static rate plus a departure whose prior decays as an Omori law (10 mm/yr at the block's sites in the first year, c = 10 yr) and is correlated in time on the logarithmic clock, as the afterslip states are. The departures are estimated with the static rotations from the 1997–2011 windows and then re-solved in every window, and each window's kinematic slip rates follow its own rotation. The transient goes into the departures (8–11 mm/yr in the first month, 2–4 mm/yr in 2006–2015, 1–2 mm/yr after 2018) instead of the static rotations, which move 2–3 mm/yr back west. In a noise-free synthetic test, 6 mm/yr of a planted 10 mm/yr decaying block motion went into the static rotations without departures; with them, 97–99% of it was recovered in every window. Window cross-validation is 10.5 above 0039's. It can't separate the two block answers: it scores the held-out windows with the same correlated noise that makes island-wide offsets cheap. The correlated-noise level of 0038–0040 (1.4 times each window's noise sd) is the cross-validation optimum for all three (0.35–5.6 tested).

Within an experiment the mesh geometry is the same in every window. The new-Noto-mesh experiment and those built on it use an updated mesh set: the Noto–Niigata mesh reaches 25 km instead of 15 km, and the BEL sheet is at 30 km instead of 50 km (trimmed under the Japan Trench forearc from 0012 on, remeshed with the same outline in 0014, 0015 and 0018–0021, limited to north of 34.63°N in 0016, and to 35.5–41.5°N in 0017). 0020 also remeshes the Nankai Trough mesh, and 0021 both trench meshes.

Uncertainty and convergence

The posterior SD of each component is computed from all MCMC draws (two chains × 300 draws). For the magnitude, the SD is that of the per-draw magnitude. These are exploratory runs: split R-hat is above 1.1 for some parameters in about half the windows, mostly the eigenmode coefficients of meshes in elastic mode in coseismic and postseismic windows. Windows with poor convergence are marked on the timeline and in the readout. Longer runs are planned.
